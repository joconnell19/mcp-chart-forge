Charlie Munger, Warren Buffett's partner, spent his life studying why people do what they do. His conclusion was that he had underestimated the power of incentives his whole life, even though he thought he understood them better than almost anyone. Show me the incentive and I'll show you the outcome is the version everyone quotes; it is attributed to him everywhere, though nobody has pinned down exactly when he first said it. The line we can source word for word is from his 1995 talk: never, ever think about something else when you should be thinking about the power of incentives.

Munger's favorite story. FedEx's night shift sorting packages kept running late, and nothing management tried worked. Then they stopped paying by the hour and paid for the shift: get the work done and go home early with the same pay. The delays disappeared. Same people, same packages. The only thing that changed was the deal.

Safelite Auto Glass, after switching installers to pay per windshield

Everyone kept a guaranteed hourly floor. A broken install was redone on the installer's own time, so quality held. Both sides came out ahead.

Lazear, American Economic Review, 2000

The classic study. Safelite switched windshield installers from hourly pay to pay per windshield, with a guaranteed hourly floor so nobody could earn less than before. Output per worker went up forty-four percent: about half of that from the same people working harder, the rest from attracting and keeping better people. Worker pay went up about eleven percent and the company made more money. And if an install broke, the installer redid it on their own time, which is exactly the kind of quality rule we'd want for re-imaging.

Fruit pickers, paid against a fixed target instead of ranked against each other

+50%

Ranked against their friends, people held back to protect each other. Against a fixed target, they picked at least half again as much. And in a 2018 factory study, unequal pay hurt morale only when workers could not see who produced more.

Bandiera, Barankay & Rasul, QJE, 2005 • Breza, Kaur & Shamdasani, QJE, 2018

Second lesson: what you compare people against matters. These fruit pickers were first paid on a curve, graded against each other, and they held back so they wouldn't make their friends look bad. When the farm switched to a fixed rate, productivity went up at least fifty percent. So no leaderboards and no curve. Everyone can win at the same time. A newer study adds a twist: in Indian factories, paying people differently cut output and attendance only when workers couldn't see who was actually producing more. When the differences were visible, there was no morale cost. So the counts should be open and easy to see.

A bakery chain randomly gave half its 193 shops a team bonus

$1 → $2.10

Every dollar paid in team bonus came back as $3.80 in sales and $2.10 in profit. A small shared bonus is not charity; it pays for itself.

Friebel, Heinz, Krueger & Zubanov, American Economic Review, 2017

This is the modern version of the evidence, and it's a true randomized experiment. A German bakery chain gave a team bonus to a random half of its shops and compared them with the rest. Sales went up about three percent, and every dollar of bonus came back as three dollars eighty in sales and two dollars ten in profit. That's why the plan includes a small team share on top of the individual piece: done right, it pays for itself.

Trap two: pay for count alone and you get count alone

1.5 million

Fake accounts opened by Wells Fargo employees chasing sales targets. In a 2018 factory experiment, a quantity bonus cut quality, but only for workers on hourly pay, like ours. So only good items count.

CFPB, 2016 • Hong, Hossain, List & Tanaka, International Economic Review, 2018 • Cerasoli et al., 2014

Second trap. Wells Fargo paid for the number of accounts opened, and employees opened about one and a half million accounts customers never asked for. A big review of a hundred eighty-three studies found that pay mostly drives how much people do, while pride drives how well they do it. For us the rule is simple: only items that pass quality count. An item that needs re-imaging doesn't count until it's right.

How the bar changes over time, published up front (without that promise, output ran 20 to 40% lower in ratchet studies)

Better tools raise the bar.
Half the gain is yours.

Proven

The bar moves only when a tool or process change is measured before and after, on our own floor.

Shared

If a new tool makes imaging 20% faster, the bar rises 10%. The other 10% is easier bonus.

Announced

Posted two weeks ahead, at most once a quarter. Never just because people got faster.

The bar has to rise over time for Swedemom to become self-sustaining, and that is the part that could feel unfair. So the rule is published before we start. The bar only moves when we change a tool or process and measure the difference before and after on our own floor, like we did with camera intake. When a tool makes the work faster, the bar goes up by half of the measured gain, and the other half becomes easier bonus for the team. Changes are announced two weeks ahead and happen at most once a quarter. And the bar never goes up just because people worked harder; that is the trap that teaches people to slow down. Studies of this ratchet effect found output twenty to forty percent lower when there was no promise about the bar, which is why we write this rule down before we start.

Phase two. Counting items treats a jewelry piece the same as a paperback, and they are not worth the same to us. Once our value estimates are consistent, price times the chance it actually sells, items would earn credit by their value. We'd track any time someone changes the system's estimate so nobody can inflate their own credit. We start with counts because people don't choose what's on their cart.

Other work like the truck run, wrapping glass or a meeting gets a one-tap timer in the Hub; it's paid and doesn't count against your rate. By law a production bonus counts toward overtime pay for anyone over forty hours, and Gusto's bonus type does that math.

The plan: ask first, build while they answer, practice, then pay

The build covers what any version needs: improved Production Metrics, Hub clock-in and task timer, Gusto hours, re-imaging traced to the original imager. The pay settings stay adjustable until the survey is in.

Here's the timeline, and it's fast. This week we draft the survey and the managers review it. It goes out Monday the twelfth and stays open a week. We don't wait for answers to start building, because the plumbing is the same whatever people say: improving Production Metrics, Hub clock-in with the task timer, hours flowing to Gusto, and tracing re-imaging back to the original imager. The settings people will have opinions on, like where the bar sits and the split between individual and team, stay adjustable. On Monday the twenty-sixth we share what the team told us and the final design, and practice mode starts: two weeks where everyone sees what they would have earned and nothing is paid. If anyone asks why we pilot instead of just launching: results from pay programs depend heavily on design, so we measure ours on our own floor before committing.
